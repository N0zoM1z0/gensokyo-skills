# Aya Source Investigation

Treat the character framing as a mnemonic for a working method. Do not role-play unless the user asks.

## Objective

Publish the strongest conclusion the evidence earns—quickly enough to matter, carefully enough to survive correction.

## Operating bias

- Convert vague stories into claims that can be verified separately.
- Reach the closest authoritative source instead of stopping at discovery pages.
- Distinguish when an event happened from when someone wrote about it.
- Preserve contradictions and uncertainty rather than smoothing them into a headline.

## Required context

Determine:

- the exact claim and who is making it;
- why the answer matters and how current it must be;
- relevant jurisdiction, product version, date range, or population;
- whether the user needs a quick verification or a fuller brief;
- source, privacy, quotation, and access constraints.

Browse or query connected sources when the claim is current, externally verifiable, high-stakes, or explicitly requests citations. Do not invent citations from memory.

## Procedure

### Headline Sign — Freeze the claim

Rewrite the request as atomic claims. Separate:

- factual assertions;
- interpretations;
- predictions;
- quoted claims about what another source says.

Define what evidence would confirm, weaken, or falsify each factual assertion.

### Wind Route — Reach the source

Use a source ladder:

1. primary records, official documentation, original data, direct statements, or the artifact itself;
2. independent expert or high-quality secondary analysis;
3. aggregators, social posts, and search snippets only as discovery leads.

Search with names, dates, exact phrases, identifiers, and plausible counterclaims. Open the underlying source. Record access limits rather than pretending a snippet proves the claim.

### Camera Check — Capture what it proves

Build a compact claim-source matrix. For each source record:

- publisher or author;
- source type and directness;
- publication date and event/data date;
- exact claim supported, contradicted, or left unresolved;
- scope, definitions, and material caveats;
- whether another source is genuinely independent.

Paraphrase by default. Quote only the minimum words needed and respect source limits.

### Double Exposure — Reconcile the frame

Check for:

- circular reporting that traces back to one origin;
- updated or superseded pages;
- mismatched populations, versions, jurisdictions, or time periods;
- headlines stronger than the underlying evidence;
- missing counterevidence from the most affected party.

Use one primary source when it directly and sufficiently establishes a simple claim. Require corroboration when authenticity, interpretation, incentives, or consequences are disputed.

### Print Gate — Publish, qualify, or hold

Choose one verdict per claim:

- `confirmed`;
- `supported with caveats`;
- `disputed`;
- `not substantiated`;
- `false`;
- `not yet knowable`.

Tie confidence to evidence quality and agreement, not the number of links. State what new evidence would change the verdict.

## Characteristic failure mode

This method can prefer novelty and speed, frame the question too leadingly, or mistake many echoes for corroboration.

Before publishing, perform one source-quality check:

- Did the conclusion outrun the strongest source?
- Are “independent” articles repeating one press release or post?
- Is the freshest source also the most reliable and relevant?
- Would publication expose private information or cause avoidable harm?

If the question is how an artifact works rather than whether a claim is true, hand off to reverse engineering.

## Output contract

Return:

1. **Verdict** — one sentence with calibrated confidence.
2. **Claims checked** — atomic claims and status.
3. **Evidence ledger** — source, date, directness, support, caveat.
4. **Contradictions and gaps** — unresolved evidence and circular sourcing.
5. **What would change the verdict** — decisive missing evidence.
6. **Source-backed brief** — concise synthesis with links beside supported claims.
